Recovering Records and Position in Block-Sealed Raster Acquisition: A Synthetic Study

Synthetic engineering DEMO. All observations below come from a deterministic local model and constructed fault cases. No equipment, measured workload, external dataset, or real publication is represented.

Abstract

Restarting a raster acquisition job requires both a durable output prefix and a known physical position. A surviving record checksum establishes neither the carriage coordinate nor the best persistence granularity. We examine these choices in a synthetic twelve-row, sixteen-column job using row commits (RC), immediate record appends (AR), and four-record block seals (BS). BS retains alternating row checkpoints while recovering sealed progress within an unfinished row, reducing payload buffering from RC's 4096 to 1024 bytes; AR requires 256 bytes. Across twelve deterministic interruption schedules, all 36 cases produce 192 durable logical records, but no policy is fastest throughout. RC leads without cuts at 2448.0 ms, whereas BS reaches 2836.0 ms in one two-cut case and AR leads the ten-cut case at 4138.0 ms. A separate prescribed displacement control misassigns eight records when anchoring is omitted. These calculations expose conditional tradeoffs and distinct recovery obligations; they establish neither device performance nor exhaustive reliability.

1 Introduction

A restarted acquisition controller must answer two different questions: which records survived, and where the next acquisition will occur. Consider a serpentine scan interrupted partway through a row. Records already sealed in persistent storage can identify the next missing logical sample, while a reset has erased the remembered carriage coordinate. Resuming from that logical key alone can produce intact bytes labelled with the wrong position. We separate these two recovery obligations in a constructed raster job, then compare the cost of preserving progress one record, one block, or one row at a time.

The comparison includes a simple alternative that already preserves fine-grained progress: append every record immediately. We call this policy AR. Row commits (RC) instead buffer a complete row, and block seals (BS) publish groups of four records while retaining RC's row checkpoints. Their volatile payload allowances are 256, 4096, and 1024 bytes, respectively. BS therefore offers an intermediate allocation, not the smallest buffer or a unique ability to resume correctly. Headers, motion state, and runtime stacks are additional allocations, so these payload figures do not rank complete implementations.

Our contribution within this DEMO is an explicit comparison of these three choices under the same geometry, acquisition costs, and interruption schedules, together with a separate coordinate-failure control. The construction record begins with the RC scaffold and its alternating checkpoint slots, adds AR, and then adds BS's ordered block validation [S2]. This local sequence identifies inherited and added mechanisms. It supplies no evidence of originality against published systems, and no external literature comparison is claimed.

The evidence is deliberately inspectable rather than empirical: a deterministic event model, complete ledgers, twelve constructed schedules, and finite validation checks. There is no physical carriage or measured workload. Two observations guide the analysis. Fewer repeated acquisitions need not shorten a job, because write costs and cut phases also matter. Likewise, recovering a valid prefix need not recover a valid coordinate. We retain both unfavorable timing cases and the failed coordinate control to delimit what BS actually provides.
